Life after the last game

Top 10 for Citadel seniors leaving football this month

  1. Grieve it on purpose.

    Name the loss out loud — the locker room, the schedule, the identity. Athletes who skip this stall out for a year. One honest conversation with a teammate or you beats six months of pretending.

  2. Write the “who am I without the helmet” statement.

    One paragraph: values, strengths, how they want to be described at 30. The Corps already gave them the material — discipline, leading under pressure, finishing hard things.

  3. Lock the 90-day plan.

    Specific dates: commissioning or job start, where they’ll live, what they’ll do the first Monday after the last game. Vague = drift.

  4. Translate the resume.

    “Starting linebacker” means nothing to a hiring manager. “Led a 30-man unit, 20+ hrs/week, under a four-year performance review” does. Same for cadet rank and duties.

  5. Work the network now, not in May.

    Ten conversations before graduation: alumni, former players in their target field, coaches’ contacts. Ask for advice, not jobs — jobs follow.

  6. Build a replacement training routine.

    Strength and conditioning was handed to them for 18 years. Pick a goal (race, lift number, body-comp target), schedule it, protect it. Movement is their mental-health insurance.

  7. Audit habits that football was covering for.

    Sleep, alcohol, screen time, spending. Structure was doing the work; without it, small cracks widen fast. Decide the rules before the structure disappears.

  8. Get financially literate before the first paycheck.

    Budget, emergency fund, TSP/401(k) match, how an O-1 pay stub or a first salary actually breaks down. Thirty minutes now saves years.

  9. Find their new team.

    Men who leave a team and don’t join another one — a unit, a church, a training group, a volunteer crew — are the ones who struggle. Belonging is non-negotiable.

  10. Keep one coach in their corner.

    A mentor or performance coach they check in with monthly through the first year out. Transition is the most dangerous window they’ll face, and nobody should run it solo.
